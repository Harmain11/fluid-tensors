One Frozen Edit, Four Ways to Ask

Prompt-Conditioned Functional Effects in a Transformer. A closer look at 1,024 archived measurements, four main research figures and two diagnostic plots.

THE FINDING IN ONE SENTENCE

The same historical transformer edit had a +0.206 functional contrast under original wording, but −0.021 after a rewrite—and the contrast is measured on attenuation, not answer correctness.

Why this question matters

Weight edits are sometimes described as if a small, localized change gives a model a new stable behavior. But the same fixed parameters process very different token sequences when the prompt is rewritten. If an edit seems to work under one phrasing, how much of the effect is tied to the prompt itself?

My wider Fluid Tensors experiments explored localized edits, activation pathways, and later repair strategies. Many of those broader repair attempts did not establish reliable task-level benefit. For this first public paper, I isolated a narrower result that can be checked numerically.

What was actually measured?

We analyzed a frozen, archived factorial matrix from one Qwen2.5-1.5B model/edit setting. Every cell records an attenuation response associated with a historical edit condition. The principal comparison is the response under a combined math-and-format instruction minus the response under a generic instruction, holding the question, wording and edit direction fixed.

32Question units
2Directions
4Wording variants
1,024Archived cells

The directions are a historical signed vector and one rotated_90 control. Wording variants are original, reworded, formal and terse; each was tested across generic, math-only, format-only and math-plus-format templates. These are 32 paired question units, not 1,024 independent sampled questions.

How did we audit the table?

Each record is keyed by question ID, edit direction, wording and instruction template. The release code checks the SHA-256 checksum of the frozen source; rejects duplicate, absent and non-finite cells; verifies all 32 × 2 × 4 × 4 combinations; and confirms prompt-token counts for matching conditions across directions. Only after those checks does it recompute contrasts and figures.

Let A(q,d,v,t) be the archived attenuation for question q, direction d, wording v, and template t. We compute Δ(q,d,v) = A(q,d,v,math_format) − A(q,d,v,generic). The main estimate is the average across 32 questions of Δ(q,signed,original) − Δ(q,signed,reworded). This is a difference of two within-question, within-direction contrasts; it is not an answer score or a probability of successful repair.

Figure 1 — The original wording stands apart

For the signed historical direction, the mean math-plus-format versus generic attenuation contrast is +0.205542 under the original wording. The same comparison is −0.020673 after rewording, −0.045819 in the formal variant and −0.002337 in the terse variant. These are averages over the same 32 question IDs, not four unrelated question sets.

The visual contrast is notable because the underlying historical edit is held fixed in the recorded panel. What changes across the comparison is the input phrasing and its associated instruction-template presentation. Importantly, prompt semantics, total length and token positions are not separately randomized.

Figure 2 — What happens to a rotated control?

We repeated the same contrast for a direction rotated 90° from the signed vector in the historical setup. Under the original wording, its mean is −0.023924 rather than the signed direction's +0.205542. Across the other three wording families, both directions exhibit varying, generally smaller contrasts. This is useful comparative evidence, but one rotated direction is not a formal random-control distribution.

Figure 3 — All 32 question-level differences

For each question, we subtracted the signed/reworded functional contrast from the signed/original contrast. The mean paired difference is +0.226216, with median +0.241814. All 32 of 32 individual differences were positive in this frozen question panel, but the magnitudes span +0.008832 to +0.712422. That spread is worth showing: it prevents an aggregate bar from implying identical behavior for every question.

A 20,000-draw nonparametric bootstrap, resampling the 32 questions with replacement, produces the percentile 95% interval [+0.184256, +0.274149] for this paired mean (seed: 20261116). This describes conditional question-sampling variability. It does not cover uncertainty from how the edit, the prompt variants or the questions were historically selected.

Figure 4 — Look at the whole response matrix

The 1,024 cells are not just four summary bars. We can inspect each instruction template inside each wording family for both directions. These two heatmaps average the raw attenuation across 32 questions for every wording-by-template cell. They make the factorial design visible, including patterns that a single math-plus-format versus generic contrast might hide.

The heatmaps help identify where the archival interaction resides, but they do not tell us whether an effect is semantic, a length artifact, a token-position interaction or a specific computational circuit. Resolving those explanations would require prospectively designed controls.

Why this is not a reasoning breakthrough

Not measured: whether the answer to a math question became correct. Not demonstrated: general-purpose repair, a unique internal mechanism, independent edit transfer, or a phase transition.
  • Prompt-length confound. In the original-wording panel, generic prompts averaged 100.25 tokens, versus 114.25 for math-plus-format. The template changes add 14 or 15 tokens across wording families. Semantics, length and token positions changed together.
  • Historical edit selection. The intervention was selected in earlier work. Its full independent provenance has not been reconstructed in the release package.
  • One model/edit context. The reanalysis covers one Qwen2.5-1.5B setup, not fresh edits on multiple model families.
  • Original table replay versus newer GPU run. The original Python script checks the archived CSV. The separate November 18 GPU run replayed 128 original values and measured 64 further questions on the same pinned model/edit.
  • Negative nearby evidence. Other Fluid Tensors evaluations did not show reliable math-correctness improvement or broad autonomous repair. Those results cannot be replaced by a larger attenuation contrast.

Supplementary plots — uncertainty and the unresolved confound

Two additional graphics show diagnostics that are easy to overlook when only the main result is shown. They are included in the full paper appendix and regenerated by the same public plotting script.

How should the figures be read together?

Figure 1 identifies a wording-conditioned mean attenuation contrast; Figure 2 asks whether that contrast looks similar for a rotated control; Figure 3 shows the distribution of paired observations rather than hiding behind a single mean; and Figure 4 displays the broader factorial measurements. Figure S1 checks the resampling arithmetic, while Figure S2 makes clear why the recorded contrast is not evidence that wording semantics alone caused the difference.

Even the consistent 32/32 positive sign pattern should be interpreted only within the historically chosen edit and archived question set. A true independent test would pre-register the selection process and evaluate held-out question types with several separately constructed directions and matched-length placebos.

November 18 update: a fresh GPU confirmation

The original paper's first result was an audit of a historical 32-question factorial table. We have now completed an additional GPU experiment with the same frozen Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model, pinned revision and rank-four layer-19 intervention. This added 64 question identities selected outside the historical exclusion registry, plus a newly executed 128-cell replay of the original contrast. The study archived 1,152 complete measurements and passed every recorded numerical-quality gate.

Why is this useful? It asks whether the original prompt-dependent response is visible on different question identities, rather than only in the archived examples. It also tests what happens when the input token counts are equalized and when the weight modification is rotated to an equally strong but orthogonal direction.

NEW CONFIRMATION RESULT / SAME MODEL

On the 64 new questions, the token-count-matched signed-edit interaction was +0.193491 (95% question-bootstrap interval [+0.166896, +0.219002]), positive on 62 out of 64 questions. The orthogonal control averaged +0.004060 and its interval [−0.004614, +0.012629] included zero.

The unadjusted signed-edit contrast on the new cohort was +0.229246. Matching reduced its mean by 0.035755, or about 15.6% of the raw mean. Thus the effect survived this specific matching operation, but the magnitude changed: formatting matters. The original 32-question contrast replayed at +0.226216, with all 128 relevant archived measurements reproduced exactly in the recorded GPU environment.

What remains unresolved: These additional questions were from the GSM8K train split, so pretraining exposure is unknown even though historical experiment identities were excluded. This is not a new model family, independent laboratory or independently selected edit. Nor is the statistic a score of how many math answers were correct. It measures the geometry of the model's output-logit response to a weight edit. The original and new results should not be pooled as identical protocols because length matching and cohort selection differ.

We release a dated v1.2 reading copy, the full methods and limits, and the hashed raw GPU study archive. The earlier Zenodo versions and their DOI histories are retained; this confirmation was conducted afterwards.

What would convince me next?

A prospective test should freeze the edit before selecting evaluation prompts, use multiple independently trained directions and model families, add token-length- and position-matched semantic placebos, and score both the internal functional response and blinded final-answer correctness. Causal claims would need direct activation mediation and stronger random/rotated controls.

The practical research lesson is modest but useful: if a weight edit's claimed benefit depends on one prompt wording, evaluation should expose that dependence before calling the edit a repair.

This paper does not claim the first discovery of prompt sensitivity. Its contribution is a bounded factorial record and transparent source-table reanalysis.
